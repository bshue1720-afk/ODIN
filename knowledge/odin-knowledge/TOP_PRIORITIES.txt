PRIORITY #1 — BUYER/CLIENT ONBOARDING (0% complete)
Insight: Speed to Value — The Golden Path
The single most predictive retention signal is whether a customer wins in Week 1.
Dan's research across his portfolio: clients who hit their first milestone in 7 days
stay 6+ months. Those who don't: 80% churn by day 30. The "golden path" is the
shortest possible route from signup to first win — strip everything else.

Why it matters for ODIN:
Onboarding is at 0%. There is no structured handoff from lead → closed deal → active
client. Without this, every won deal starts with confusion, and confusion = churn.
The golden path principle means ODIN needs ONE defined sequence that fires
immediately on deal close and walks the new buyer to their first clear win.

Implementation:
1. Define the "first win" for a real estate buyer: receiving their first deal alert
   that matches their buy box with full deal summary.
2. Build a 7-day onboarding sequence in ODIN: Day 0 welcome (SMS+email), Day 1 buy
   box confirmation request, Day 3 first deal match alert, Day 7 check-in ping.
3. Add a `onboarding_stage` field to the buyers table (0–7 day tracker).
4. Trigger a Slack alert to Brock/Eddie if a buyer goes 3 days without engagement.
Source: "How I Built a $100M Business that Never Loses Customers"

PRIORITY #2 — BUYER/CLIENT ONBOARDING (0% complete)
Insight: Dummy-Proof the Product — Adoption Ladder
Most churn is not dissatisfaction — it's confusion. Clients don't fail because the
product is bad; they fail because they never fully adopt it. The adoption ladder
maps every feature to a milestone. You guide clients up the ladder one rung at a time
instead of dumping everything on them at once.

Why it matters for ODIN:
New buyers need to understand: (a) how deals get sent to them, (b) how to respond,
(c) what happens after they express interest. If any of those steps are unclear,
they go silent and you never know if they're disengaged or just confused.

Implementation:
1. Create a 3-step adoption ladder for buyers: Step 1 = receive deal alert,
   Step 2 = reply with interest/pass, Step 3 = get connected with seller/agent.
2. ODIN should track which step each buyer is on and send a re-engagement nudge
   if they stall (e.g., 2 deals sent, 0 replies = trigger "how are we doing?" SMS).
3. Build a buyer engagement score (0–100) based on open rate, reply rate, and
   deals reviewed. Flag low-score buyers for manual outreach.
Source: "How I Built a $100M Business that Never Loses Customers"

The most high-leverage leadership behavior is a weekly meeting cadence where every
metric has a DRI (Directly Responsible Individual), traffic light status (green/
yellow/red), and a trend line. The founder reviews, asks one question per red metric,
and trusts the team to solve it. This replaces firefighting with systemic awareness.

Why it matters for ODIN:
Voice Briefing is at 10% — the concept exists but isn't delivering a real briefing.
A voice briefing is essentially a scorecard sync delivered verbally. Once ODIN has
its KPI system built (Priority #5), the voice briefing becomes the delivery layer:
every morning ODIN should speak the scorecard to Brock.

Implementation:
1. Define 5–7 ODIN metrics that matter daily: leads in pipeline, deals under offer,
   buyer responses pending, follow-up tasks overdue, revenue this month vs. target.
2. ODIN pulls these at 7am, formats them as a 60-second spoken summary.
3. Deliver via Slack voice message or a scheduled SMS digest (text-based briefing
   is acceptable as interim until full voice is built).
4. Each metric must have a DRI (even if it's just ODIN itself for automated ones).
Source: "Build a Business That Runs Itself", "Leadership Rhythm" frameworks

PRIORITY #4 — DECISION QUEUE (20% complete)
Insight: The 1-3-1 Rule — Eliminate Founder Bottlenecks
The #1 reason businesses stall: everything routes back to the founder for a decision.
1-3-1 Rule: any time a team member brings a problem, they must bring (1) the problem
statement, (3) possible solutions they've considered, and (1) their recommendation.
The founder only approves or declines — never invents solutions.

Why it matters for ODIN:
Decision Queue is at 20%. Currently decisions pile up with no structure. When Eddie
or the system surfaces something that needs Brock's call (e.g., pricing a deal,
handling an unusual seller response), it should arrive pre-packaged with context
and a recommendation — not as a raw question.

Implementation:
1. Add a `decision_queue` table: id, issue_summary, option_1, option_2, option_3,
   recommended_option, status (pending/approved/declined), created_at.
2. Any agent or automation that hits a decision point creates a queue entry instead
   of pinging Brock with a question.
3. Build a `/decisions` Slack command that shows Brock pending items in 1-3-1 format.
4. Auto-close decisions older than 48 hours by executing the recommended option
   and logging the auto-approval (with Slack alert).
Source: "27 Years of No Bullsh*t Business Advice", "How to Build a Business That Runs Itself"

PRIORITY #5 — DECISION QUEUE (20% complete)
Insight: The $50-to-Fix-It Rule — Autonomous Problem Resolution
For any recurring problem that costs less than $50 to fix, the team (or system)
should fix it without asking. This single rule eliminates 80% of founder interrupts.
Applied to automation: if a retry, a lookup, or a fallback costs nothing or nearly
nothing, ODIN should execute it automatically and log the decision — never surface it.

Why it matters for ODIN:
ODIN currently escalates things that shouldn't require human judgment. Every time
ODIN pings Brock with something it could handle itself, it's burning attention.
The $50-equivalent threshold for ODIN: anything that doesn't commit money or change
a deal status should be handled autonomously.

Implementation:
1. Define ODIN's autonomous action rules: retry failed SMS (auto), switch to email
   fallback if SMS bounces (auto), re-queue stalled lead after 7 days (auto),
   send buyer a "still interested?" ping after 14 days of silence (auto).
2. All autonomous actions are logged to an `autonomous_actions` table with timestamp,
   action taken, and outcome.
3. Weekly digest to Brock showing what ODIN handled without him (builds trust
   in the system and surfaces patterns for further automation).
Source: "Buy Back Your Time", "27 Years of No Bullsh*t Business Advice"

Why it matters for ODIN:
KPI Visibility at 40% means some data is tracked but no structured scorecard exists.
Without this, it's impossible to know which APEX dimension needs attention most —
and weekly leadership rhythm is impossible without a scorecard to sync against.

   follow-up completion rate: target 90%, buyer response rate: target 40%,
   deals under contract: target 2/month, SMS deliverability: target 95%+).
2. Build a `scorecard` table: metric_name, dri, target, actual_this_week,
   actual_last_week, status (green/yellow/red).
3. Auto-populate via existing ODIN data jobs (heartbeat jobs already running).
4. `/scorecard` Slack command outputs the full scorecard in formatted text.

Insight: Theory of Constraints — Fix the Bottleneck, Not Everything
In any system, one constraint limits total throughput. Improving anything else
gives zero gain until the constraint is fixed. For sales pipelines: identify which
stage has the lowest conversion rate. That's the constraint. Fix THAT before
touching anything else.

Why it matters for ODIN:
With KPI Visibility at 40%, ODIN can't yet identify its own constraint. Once the
scorecard is built (Priority #8), the next step is running a constraint analysis:
which pipeline stage loses the most leads? That stage gets all improvement energy.

Implementation:
1. Add conversion rate tracking per pipeline stage (Lead→Qualified, Qualified→Offer,
   Offer→Under Contract, Under Contract→Closed).
2. Identify the lowest conversion rate stage — this is the current constraint.
3. All ODIN development sprints start with the constraint stage until it improves
   by 10 percentage points, then re-identify constraint and repeat.
4. Display current constraint prominently in the ODIN dashboard.
Source: "Business is Hard Until You Build These Systems", "These 4 AI Automations"

Why it matters for ODIN:

PRIORITY #12 — FOLLOW-UP AUTOMATION (60% complete)
Insight: The Multi-Touch Follow-Up Sequence — 7 Touches, Multiple Channels
Data from Dan's portfolio: 80% of sales happen between touch 4 and touch 12.
Most people quit after touch 2. The winning follow-up system uses multiple channels
(SMS, email, voicemail drop, social) with decreasing frequency over time.
Structure: Day 1, 3, 7, 14, 30, 60, 90.

Why it matters for ODIN:
Follow-Up at 60% means sequences exist but aren't complete. The 7-touch, multi-
channel structure is likely missing. Sellers who don't respond after the first
contact are the highest-potential leads — they just need more touches.

Implementation:
1. Audit current follow-up sequence: how many touches? Which channels? What cadence?
2. Expand to 7-touch minimum: SMS (Day 1, 7, 30), Email (Day 3, 14, 60),
   Personal ping from Brock or Eddie (Day 90 only for highest-scored leads).
3. Add channel fallback: if SMS fails, auto-switch to email. If email bounces,
   flag for manual outreach.
4. Track "touch number at first response" — this data tells you where leads actually
   convert and lets you optimize sequence timing.
Source: "27 Years of Sales Advice", "These 4 AI Automations"

PRIORITY #13 — OFFER GENERATION (70% complete)
Insight: 10-80-10 Rule — Founder Sets the Frame, System Executes the Middle
The founder owns the first 10% (strategy, criteria, constraints) and the last 10%
(integration, approval, relationship). The middle 80% is execution — repeatable,
delegatable, automatable. Applied to offers: Brock defines the buy box and profit
targets (10%), ODIN calculates and drafts the offer (80%), Brock reviews and sends (10%).

Why it matters for ODIN:
Offer Generation at 70% is close. The likely gap: ODIN calculates numbers but doesn't
draft the full offer communication. The 10-80-10 rule clarifies the target state —
Brock should only touch offers at the beginning (criteria) and end (approval).

Implementation:
1. Define Brock's 10% inputs for offers: ARV source, repair cost estimate, max
   offer formula (e.g., 70% ARV minus repairs minus assignment fee target).
2. ODIN's 80%: pull ARV from existing data or web lookup, apply formula, draft
   full offer letter with seller name, property address, and offer amount.
3. Brock's 10%: review draft in Slack, approve with a single button/command.
4. On approval, ODIN sends the offer via configured channel (SMS/email/DocuSign link).
Source: "How to Build a Business That Runs Itself", "Build a Business That Runs Itself"

Insight: SMS Advantage — 30% Higher Show Rates, Real-Time Engagement
Text message open rates: 98% vs. 20% for email. Response time: 90 seconds vs. 90
minutes. For appointment confirmation specifically: SMS reminders sent 24 hours and

SMS confirmation, reminder, and follow-up — without exception.

Why it matters for ODIN:
SMS Outbound at 70% means the infrastructure exists but the full sequence isn't

   to reschedule?" within 15 minutes. Two follow-up attempts at 24hr and 72hr.
3. Add SMS deliverability tracking to the scorecard (delivery rate, response rate).
4. Test personalization tokens in SMS (first name, property address) — personal
   messages get significantly higher response rates.

PRIORITY #15 — LEAD QUALIFICATION (80% complete)
Insight: MCTP Scoring — Motivation, Condition, Timeline, Price as Qualification Matrix
The four dimensions of lead quality: Motivation (why are they selling?), Condition
(what's the property state?), Timeline (how urgent?), Price (are they flexible?).
A lead only moves to offer stage when all four are scored. Leads missing Motivation
data are 10x less likely to close — motivation is the #1 predictor.

Why it matters for ODIN:
Lead Qualification at 80% is the most complete APEX dimension, but 80% means
something is still missing. The likely gap: Motivation is not being captured or
weighted correctly. Most systems capture price flexibility but skip deep motivation
probing — that's where qualified leads get miscategorized.

Implementation:
1. Audit current qualification fields: does ODIN capture all 4 MCTP dimensions?
   Which fields are missing or inconsistently filled?
2. Add a Motivation score field (1–5): 1 = browsing/curious, 5 = must sell now.
   Weight this 2x in the overall lead score formula.
3. Build a qualification gap alert: if a lead is 30 days old and Motivation is
   still unscored, trigger an ODIN task to capture it before the lead goes cold.
4. Update the AI qualifier's question sequence to probe Motivation first, before
   price — this mirrors the doctor frame (diagnose before prescribing).
Source: Rick Ginn MCTP framework + Dan Martell qualification frameworks
